# EDU090 The Instruction Card Opens One Step

**Catalogue family:** Learning, accessibility/inclusion (Appendix D, Learning accessibility/inclusion).  
**Status:** idea. Not a completed script, not a rendered catalogue treatment, no audience study. Learning claims remain unverified until a claim-level check. Originality means authored for the catalogue, not an exhaustive prior-art search. The current story-first review may reject or substantially rewrite this premise.

## Premise (authored wording)

A participant wants to assemble a harmless paper model, but a dense paragraph buries the current action among later steps. A plain-language sequence with optional detail exposes one actionable step at a time.

**Hook:** Which action should happen first?

**Visible action:** A paragraph separates into numbered action cards; an optional-detail tab expands without displacing the current step.

**Intended takeaway or theme:** Clear sequencing and optional detail can reduce unnecessary interpretation demands.

**Gate:** Test with intended users; plain language is context-dependent and does not guarantee universal comprehension.

**Evidence class:** `requires_primary_source_review`. **Original format tags:** step_by_step, side_by_side, interactive_branch, prediction_quiz, behind_the_scenes.

Factual checkability is not factual verification; the evidence class says what kind of check a claim would need, not that it was done.

- F03 separates one worked case into setup, action and a checked checkpoint.
- F04 compares initial and changed conditions while isolating one justified variable; no invented counterfactual result.
- F05 starts at the outcome, inspects clues, then replays the supported chain forward without overstating causality.

- S04 Technical exploded view (vector; block3d): Clarifies assembly and relationships. Check: Verify labels, scale and separation directions.
- S09 Architectural section (block3d; vector): Explains hidden interior relationships. Check: Keep the cutaway plane honest and distinct from a real inspection.
- S10 Field notebook (sketch): Shows curiosity and provisional learning. Check: Label illustrations and unresolved facts explicitly.

- S12 Blueprint to object (vector; block3d): Connects intent, geometry and result. Check: Only claim exact correspondence after verification.
- S14 Botanical linework (sketch; vector): Makes growth and structure readable. Check: Species-specific claims require sourced references.
- S17 Retro instruction sheet (vector): Turns a procedure into distinct, memorable checkpoints. Check: Use current verified steps; nostalgia is only surface treatment.
- S18 Motion comic (cutout; vector): Supports dialogue and clear scene transitions. Check: Keep reading order and character identity consistent.

## Story tests to apply

1. **This premise's gate:** Test with intended users; plain language is context-dependent and does not guarantee universal comprehension.
2. **Format gates:** F01 One mechanism explained: Show a single causal chain and state model limits. F02 Pause and predict: Do not use guessing to teach emergency response. F03 Worked scenario: Verify every instructional step against the chosen source. F04 Controlled contrast: Counterfactual outcomes remain hypotheses until validated. F05 Trace the cause: Sequence must support causality rather than merely correlation.
3. **Approach gates:** A01 Articulated vector evidence: Rig contacts, text layout and every diagram relationship must be checked; this is 2D animation. A02 Sketch and cutaway explanation: Verify stroke order, annotation tracking and model limits. Rough strokes must never change factual geometry.
4. **Combination rule:** Learning combinations fail if their comparison has no controlled basis, their backward trace overstates causality, or factual verification removes the premise. Every combination inherits the story risks, format gate, approach gate, rights review, caption readability, motion comfort, audio mix and exact-byte checks.
5. **Story contract** (write these sentences before drawing shots; `story_contract` schema):

   - **Want:** What does the protagonist actively want before the film ends?
   - **Stakes:** What visibly changes if they fail, and why should this particular character care?
   - **Constraint:** What prevents the obvious solution? Establish it early enough to feel fair.
   - **Choice:** What does the character do that causes the next complication?
   - **Escalation:** How does each attempt change the situation rather than reset it?
   - **Knowledge:** What does the viewer know, what does the character know, and what is still uncertain?
   - **Plant:** Which visible detail will matter later? The viewer must have a fair chance to notice it.
   - **Reversal:** How does the final action use established information in a surprising way?
   - **Consequence:** What final image shows that the character or situation has changed?

6. **Storyboard gate before a full render** (`storyboard_gate`):

   1. Can the opening promise be understood without an explanatory paragraph?
   2. Does something consequential happen before the viewer has merely watched a character travel?
   3. Can every middle beat be connected by “because” or “therefore,” rather than only “and then”?
   4. Does each attempt change the options, risk, relationship, or knowledge?
   5. Is there a fair plant for the solution and a reason the character did not use it immediately?
   6. Does the ending resolve the question raised at the beginning?
   7. Is at least one surprise specific to this premise rather than a generic stumble or shrug?
   8. Would removing a shot improve the film? Remove or rewrite shots with no new action, information, emotion, or rhythm.
   9. Does the premise sustain the intended duration? Shorten it or deepen the conflict rather than adding filler.
   10. Is the intended reaction evidenced by review, or merely hoped for? Keep that distinction visible.

7. **Production-independent story tests** on a rough animatic: Before detailed rendering, test each board as a rough animatic with disposable poses and temp sound. Ask a few people unfamiliar with the pitch:

   1. What did the main character want by the first few seconds?
   2. What would happen if they failed?
   3. Which choice made the situation worse?
   4. What changed halfway through?
   5. What earlier detail made the ending possible?
   6. Where did you first want the story to move faster?
   7. What changed for the character by the final image?

   Do not explain the intended answers before they respond. These are clarity tests, not statistically meaningful audience research. If the essential answers are missing, revise the story rather than polishing the same shots.

8. **Global kill criteria:** no concrete uncertainty in the opening; interchangeable failed attempts; a midpoint that only increases noise; an unplanted new rule solving the climax; an ending that is merely an object reveal; an achievement erased by an arbitrary reset; a punch line requiring narration to explain why it is funny. A technically clean render does not rescue any of these.

Run the whole gate with the `storyboard_before_render` command or ask the `story_doctor` subagent to apply the contract and tests to a draft.
