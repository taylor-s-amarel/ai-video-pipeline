# ENT028 The Backpack Swap

**Catalogue family:** Fiction, cooperation (Appendix D section "Fiction cooperation").  
**Status:** idea. Not a completed script, not a rendered catalogue treatment, no audience study. Fictional themes are not empirical claims. Originality means authored for the catalogue, not an exhaustive prior-art search. The current story-first review may reject or substantially rewrite this premise.

## Premise (authored wording)

Two hikers in a toy landscape cannot reach a lookout because one has a wide pack that catches on narrow arches and the other has a tall pack that hits low beams. They exchange packs at the route junction, each takes the fitting path, and meet at the overlook with the other’s picnic.

**Hook:** We brought the right bags to the wrong paths.

**Visible action:** Wide pack wedges at an arch; tall pack taps a beam; packs switch; split paths converge at a picnic spot.

**Intended takeaway or theme:** Reassigning resources can be better than forcing a fit.

**Gate:** Use toy terrain with no real climbing or packing safety advice.

**Evidence class:** `fictional_demonstration`. **Original format tags:** narrative_lesson, silent_visual, side_by_side.

Factual checkability is not factual verification: the evidence class classifies how a claim could be checked; it does not record that any check was done.

- F08 gives the viewer a fair advance clue before the protagonist notices it; no last-second coincidence.
- F09 lets readable actions and reactions carry the story with sparse captions and purposeful sound.

## Story tests to apply

1. **This premise's gate:** Use toy terrain with no real climbing or packing safety advice.
2. **Format gates:** F06 Character fable: A lesson is a theme, not evidence for a general factual claim. F07 Two-choice dilemma: Both branches must be plausible within the established world. F08 Dramatic irony: Stage clues fairly and avoid a hidden last-second solution. F09 Visual comedy chain: Every gag should follow from the previous action. F10 Object's-eye retelling: Change information and emphasis, not merely narration wording.
3. **Approach gates:** A01 Articulated vector evidence: Rig contacts, text layout and every diagram relationship must be checked; this is 2D animation. A03 Tactile miniature staging: Requires a real 3D scene, alternate-angle check, contact/shadow review and source reopening. Blockbench is a research-only asset option.
4. **Combination rule:** Fiction combinations fail if their alternative choice, advance clue or object viewpoint requires a contrived new premise rather than illuminating the existing one. Every combination inherits the story risks, format gate, approach gate, rights review, caption readability, motion comfort, audio mix and exact-byte checks.
5. **Story contract** (write these sentences before drawing shots; `story_contract` schema):

   - **Want:** What does the protagonist actively want before the film ends?
   - **Stakes:** What visibly changes if they fail, and why should this particular character care?
   - **Constraint:** What prevents the obvious solution? Establish it early enough to feel fair.
   - **Choice:** What does the character do that causes the next complication?
   - **Escalation:** How does each attempt change the situation rather than reset it?
   - **Knowledge:** What does the viewer know, what does the character know, and what is still uncertain?
   - **Plant:** Which visible detail will matter later? The viewer must have a fair chance to notice it.
   - **Reversal:** How does the final action use established information in a surprising way?
   - **Consequence:** What final image shows that the character or situation has changed?

6. **Storyboard gate before a full render** (`storyboard_gate`):

   1. Can the opening promise be understood without an explanatory paragraph?
   2. Does something consequential happen before the viewer has merely watched a character travel?
   3. Can every middle beat be connected by “because” or “therefore,” rather than only “and then”?
   4. Does each attempt change the options, risk, relationship, or knowledge?
   5. Is there a fair plant for the solution and a reason the character did not use it immediately?
   6. Does the ending resolve the question raised at the beginning?
   7. Is at least one surprise specific to this premise rather than a generic stumble or shrug?
   8. Would removing a shot improve the film? Remove or rewrite shots with no new action, information, emotion, or rhythm.
   9. Does the premise sustain the intended duration? Shorten it or deepen the conflict rather than adding filler.
   10. Is the intended reaction evidenced by review, or merely hoped for? Keep that distinction visible.

7. **Production-independent story tests** on a rough animatic: Before detailed rendering, test each board as a rough animatic with disposable poses and temp sound. Ask a few people unfamiliar with the pitch:

   1. What did the main character want by the first few seconds?
   2. What would happen if they failed?
   3. Which choice made the situation worse?
   4. What changed halfway through?
   5. What earlier detail made the ending possible?
   6. Where did you first want the story to move faster?
   7. What changed for the character by the final image?

   Do not explain the intended answers before they respond. These are clarity tests, not statistically meaningful audience research. If the essential answers are missing, revise the story rather than polishing the same shots.

8. **Global kill criteria:** no concrete uncertainty in the opening; interchangeable failed attempts; a midpoint that only increases noise; an unplanted new rule solving the climax; an ending that is merely an object reveal; an achievement erased by an arbitrary reset; a punch line requiring narration to explain why it is funny. A technically clean render does not rescue any of these.

For comedy and suspense, check the `comedy_mechanisms` table (each mechanism has a failure to reject) and the `suspense_rules`.

Run the whole gate with the `storyboard_before_render` command or ask the `story_doctor` subagent to apply the contract and tests to a draft.
