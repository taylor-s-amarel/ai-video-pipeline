# Inventory Space (timed storyboard 2 of 3)

**Status:** Proposed concepts and unrendered storyboards. These are editorial recommendations, not measured audience results or claims of user acceptance. The earlier BOXED IN film's technical progress does not establish that its story worked. The next production should pass a story test before final animation.

**Scope:** Eight new development topics: three complete timed storyboards and five additional pitches. They are separate from the earlier 240-premise catalogue and its 2,400 treatment candidates. Boards and pitches are not added together as eleven stories.

**Editorial recommendation:** **Inventory Space** is the most distinctive game-world idea, but its carry rule must survive a rough animatic test. Editorial ranking considers opening clarity, causal escalation, character agency, earned surprise, visual comedy, and production risk. It does not predict views or retention.

**Format:** approximately 88 seconds; ten shots; one compact game plaza, two NPCs and one player avatar.

**Opening image:** A red deletion boundary eats the edge of a game plaza. At the center, a couple has just set out a tiny anniversary dinner. UI: “MAP REMOVAL 01:10.” A departing player's attached bundle of gear remains solid as a nearby abandoned prop disappears.

**Dramatic question:** Can these two background characters make a speedrunner take them along before their whole map is removed?

**Characters and conflicting goals:** A repair-shop NPC wants to save his partner and their dinner. His partner, a cook, refuses to be abandoned. The player wants useful equipment without delays or dialogue. He is impatient, not deliberately cruel.

**One world rule:** Physically attached assemblies carried through the exit are preserved; things left in the old map are removed. Demonstrate this rule before the protagonists exploit it. There is no arbitrary inventory-slot loophole, newly introduced shrinking power, or special exemption for romance.

**Stakes:** Their home and the selves they have built in it will be lost. The visual tone stays comic, but the film must treat their wish to stay together as real.

**Escalation chain:** They try to attract the player with an item → he takes the item without them → the cook's genuine service draws him back → furniture is rejected as dead weight → the couple sacrifices their fixed shop to make one useful mobile assembly → the player tests it at the worst moment → the carry rule preserves them → they finally claim time for their dinner.

**Planted details:** The player can carry a visibly attached rack as one physical load; his sword's repair indicator is low; a snack visibly restores his health; the shop's removable wooden counter, heavy stone base, and transport straps are already present. These details explain both the construction and why the player wants it.

## Timed storyboard

1. **0–6 seconds. The world is going away.** Wide overhead of a cozy square with a red deletion line eating its outer paving. A couple's candlelit table sits in the remaining center. UI clearly reads “MAP REMOVAL 01:10” and, briefly, “CARRIED LOADS TRANSFER.” A player picks up a rack with a small bucket physically attached; both move together and stay solid while an abandoned matching bucket beyond the boundary dissolves. The repair NPC sees this. The cook catches their wobbling anniversary candle.

2. **6–14 seconds. An obvious sales pitch.** Tracking medium: the repair NPC runs to the player, holding up a valuable sword and pointing back at both people. The player instantly takes the sword and skips the dialogue prompt. The repair NPC catches a shoulder strap to climb along, but the player gently unhooks him and sets him back on the ground. The NPC is left with his finger still indicating “us.” Sound: a cheerful loot chime cutting off his first syllable. The player has demonstrated his priority and created the next problem: their best bargaining chip is gone.

3. **14–23 seconds. Something the player actually needs.** The player tests the new sword against a practice target. Its low durability indicator flashes. A small hit from the target knocks a little health away. The cook holds up a snack; he stops immediately, eats, and visibly heals. The repair NPC understands the opening and fixes the blade with one quick, characteristic tap. The player pauses for the first time. This establishes actual useful skills, not just a fake rarity label.

4. **23–31 seconds. Useful people, inconvenient shop.** The pair gesture toward their dinner and shop. The player tries to move their heavy counter, gets a “TOO HEAVY” response, shrugs and turns toward the exit. The cook catches the table before it tips and saves the anniversary candle. The repair NPC looks from the attached rack on the player's back to his own removable counter top, its transport straps, and the heavy stone base that does not need to travel. Their location is the obstacle; their relationship and skills are not.

5. **31–41 seconds. The costly decision.** Medium two-shot: the repair NPC unbolts the beloved shop's light wooden counter top, leaving its heavy stone base and fixed oven behind. He starts to turn the portable wood into a carrying frame. The cook stops him for a beat, touching a little hand-painted shop sign. He offers the sign to her rather than discarding it. She nods and unhooks the dinner table. They work together, turning the usable lightweight parts of their permanent shop into a compact mobile stall. Show the substantial fixed masonry they leave behind; folding alone must not magically reduce its weight. The red boundary consumes the empty chairs behind them. They have actively given up their old arrangement to preserve what matters to them.

6. **41–51 seconds. Build an honest offer.** Side view, clear silhouette: hinged counter folds; straps tighten; tiny table locks into the frame. The two NPCs sit inside the physical assembly, with their hands and faces plainly visible. Their existing shop sign now hangs on its back. They roll into the player's path. UI describes its real functions: “REPAIR + SNACKS.” Avoid invented statistics or a fraudulent “legendary” sticker. The cook offers another snack; the repair NPC presents the repaired blade.

7. **51–61 seconds. The worst possible product test.** The player lifts the whole attached assembly. It contains them exactly as the earlier carried rack contained its attached bucket. He immediately performs a dodge roll. The couple braces, the candle falls, and the cook catches it while the repair NPC saves the soup. A strained look passes between them: being useful on the road has a cost. The player sees the frame survive the test and decides to keep it.

8. **61–72 seconds. Commit to the carry.** Low tracking shot toward the exit. The deletion boundary advances behind them. A loose strap starts slipping from the frame. The repair NPC must let go of a keepsake or risk the whole assembly detaching; the cook takes the keepsake, freeing him to secure the strap. They solve the danger together. The player crosses the transfer line with the complete load at approximately 69.5 seconds, leaving half a second on the original 70-second countdown. The empty shop square disappears at 70 seconds; the remaining two seconds of this shot establish the new map. Sound drops to near silence at the crossing, then returns in the new map.

9. **72–81 seconds. They actually made it.** Calm rear three-quarter shot in the new map. The pair unfold their little table inside the carried stall and set out the saved sign and candle. The player holds up his sword for service. The cook points to the anniversary meal, then hangs a small “BACK AFTER DESSERT” sign over the service hatch. The player's hand pauses. He looks at the two faces, finally registering people rather than equipment.

10. **81–88 seconds. The speedrunner waits.** Wide: the player sits on a rock, impatient foot tapping, while the couple enjoys one quiet toast. He reaches toward a new “SKIP” prompt out of habit; it is the card on their table, reading “NO SKIPPING DESSERT.” He lowers his hand and waits. The cook passes him a small piece of cake through the hatch. His foot stops tapping. End on three little chewing sounds in an unexpectedly peaceful game world.

**Midpoint change:** They stop trying to persuade the player to preserve their old life unchanged. They combine their genuine abilities into a mobile arrangement he has reason to carry, then insist that usefulness does not eliminate their right to a moment together.

**Why watch the next beat:** Will he take them or only their possessions? Can they build an offer he values in time? Will the physical attachment hold? Once saved, will he treat them as people?

**Alternate ending:** The player reaches a difficult fight and politely rings the little service bell. The couple's sign reads “ANNIVERSARY DINNER. PLEASE QUEUE.” He joins a tiny, newly formed queue of other players. Use only if the extra people can be introduced without an unexplained time jump; otherwise keep the quieter ending.

**Obvious weakness to test:** The world rule and the construction can consume too much explanation. If the audience needs a paragraph about inventory systems, this is not ready. The initial deletion demonstration must also make the stakes emotionally intelligible without pretending to depict actual game software.

**Kill criteria before rendering:** Reject or rewrite if any viewer reasonably asks, “Why didn't they just climb onto him immediately?” without seeing that he rejects unwanted weight; if an attached assembly is only declared legal at the climax; if the portable stall cannot be understood from its silhouette; or if the ending resets the pair again and cancels their success. Preserve both characters, their meal, and the earned change in the player's behavior.

## Before any render

1. Keep the board's status at proposed until something stronger is observed. A table read, silent animatic, or owner storyboard choice can prevent a wasted full render; these are proposed review methods, not proof that a fresh audience has approved a premise.
2. `storyboard.json` follows the `timed_storyboard` schema. Check timing and required story fields with `python3 timed_storyboard_check.py storyboard.json` and print the review sheet with `--sheet`.
3. Answer the storyboard gate with shot numbers as evidence:

   1. Can the opening promise be understood without an explanatory paragraph?
   2. Does something consequential happen before the viewer has merely watched a character travel?
   3. Can every middle beat be connected by “because” or “therefore,” rather than only “and then”?
   4. Does each attempt change the options, risk, relationship, or knowledge?
   5. Is there a fair plant for the solution and a reason the character did not use it immediately?
   6. Does the ending resolve the question raised at the beginning?
   7. Is at least one surprise specific to this premise rather than a generic stumble or shrug?
   8. Would removing a shot improve the film? Remove or rewrite shots with no new action, information, emotion, or rhythm.
   9. Does the premise sustain the intended duration? Shorten it or deepen the conflict rather than adding filler.
   10. Is the intended reaction evidenced by review, or merely hoped for? Keep that distinction visible.

4. Test a rough animatic with people unfamiliar with the pitch. Do not explain the intended answers before they respond. These are clarity tests, not statistically meaningful audience research. If the essential answers are missing, revise the story rather than polishing the same shots.

   1. What did the main character want by the first few seconds?
   2. What would happen if they failed?
   3. Which choice made the situation worse?
   4. What changed halfway through?
   5. What earlier detail made the ending possible?
   6. Where did you first want the story to move faster?
   7. What changed for the character by the final image?

5. Global kill criteria: no concrete uncertainty in the opening; interchangeable failed attempts; a midpoint that only increases noise; an unplanted new rule solving the climax; an ending that is merely an object reveal; an achievement erased by an arbitrary reset; a punch line requiring narration to explain why it is funny. A technically clean render does not rescue any of these.
6. Apply this board's own kill criteria above. Use the `storyboard_before_render` command for the whole sequence and the `story_doctor` subagent for an editorial pass.
