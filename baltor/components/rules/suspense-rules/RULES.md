# Suspense rules

Source: Animation and Video Production Master Guide (v1.1, 10 October 2026), "Suspense without empty delay" (line 116).

Each sentence of the guide's section is one rule, in its original order:

1. Give the viewer a concrete question: whether a delivery will arrive before the bridge folds, whether a decoy will fool the wrong pursuer, or whether two characters realize they are solving opposite halves of the same problem.
2. Show what success and failure look like.
3. Alternate action with short opportunities to understand new information.
4. Narrow the available choices as the film proceeds.
5. A time limit should change decisions.
6. A clock that never affects behavior is decoration.
7. An apparent escape should expose a meaningful cost or reveal a new relationship.
8. Keep geography legible; uncertainty about what happens next is useful, confusion about where everyone stands usually is not.
9. Let sound anticipate a threat only when the source and payoff make sense.

From the story contract: Suspense comes from uncertainty with understandable consequences, not merely dark music or a slowly opening door.

Check on a board: write the concrete question in one sentence; point to the shots that show success and failure; list the choices still open after each shot and confirm the list narrows; for any clock, name the decision it changes; for any apparent escape, name its cost or the relationship it reveals.

Status language: an idea or proposal is not a script; a storyboard is not a render; a render is not a delivery; a technical delivery is not creative acceptance; an editorial review is not audience evidence. Record the strongest stage actually observed and never upgrade it.
